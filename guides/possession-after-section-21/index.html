Section 21 has gone. How landlords regain possession in 2026

The Section 8 grounds landlords in England now rely on after the abolition of Section 21, the notice periods, the court process and the mistakes that lose cases.

By the Sure Lets & Manage teamPublished 14 September 2026, updated 14 November 20266 min read

For a generation, Section 21 let a landlord in England end a tenancy without giving a reason. It went on 1 May 2026, for new and existing tenancies alike. Notices served before that date could still be taken to court until 31 July 2026, and that window has now closed. Landlords can still regain possession, but only on a ground in Schedule 2 of the Housing Act 1988, with the right notice, the right evidence and, if the tenant does not leave, a court order. This guide explains how it now works.

The grounds landlords actually use

There are more than twenty grounds. In practice most claims turn on a handful.

On ground 14 the claim can be issued straight away, but the court cannot make an order until at least 14 days after the notice.

A mandatory ground means the court must grant possession if the ground is proved. A discretionary ground means the court must also decide that it is reasonable to grant possession, which is where evidence and conduct matter most.

Selling or moving in: the two new mandatory grounds

Grounds 1 and 1A replaced the most common reasons landlords used Section 21. Both have conditions attached.

  • Neither can take effect in the first twelve months of the tenancy. You can serve the notice earlier, but the date in it must fall after the first anniversary.
  • Four months' notice is required.
  • After a notice on either ground you cannot re-let the property, or market it to let, from the day the notice is served until twelve months after the date given in it, with narrow exceptions. Doing so is an offence, and the former tenant can apply for a rent repayment order of up to two years' rent.
  • The court will expect evidence of intention: an estate agent's instruction, a memorandum of sale, or in the case of moving in, evidence that the family member is real and the intention genuine.

Landlords who use these grounds to remove a tenant they simply want rid of are who the new enforcement powers were written for. Councils can impose penalties of up to £7,000 for breaches of the new rules and up to £40,000, or prosecute, for offences such as knowingly misusing a ground or re-letting inside the restricted period.

Rent arrears

Ground 8 is the mandatory arrears ground. Under the Act the threshold is three months' arrears, or thirteen weeks if rent is paid weekly, and the arrears must exist both on the day the notice is served and on the day of the hearing. Arrears that exist only because a Universal Credit payment the tenant is entitled to has not yet arrived are left out of the count. A tenant who pays enough to drop below the threshold the day before court defeats the ground. That is why we serve grounds 10 and 11 alongside ground 8 on every arrears notice, so that the claim survives a partial payment.

Read our separate guide on what to do in the first 30 days of arrears, because most arrears cases are won or lost before a notice is ever served.

The process from notice to keys

  1. Check the file. Start with the deposit. Since 1 May 2026 a court can only make a possession order, on any ground except the antisocial behaviour ones, if the deposit is in an approved scheme and the prescribed information has been given, or the deposit has been returned. If that was missed you can put it right late and then proceed, but the tenant keeps a claim for up to three times the deposit. Then check the gas safety record, the electrical report, the written statement of terms and any licence. Gaps there do not bar the claim in the same way, but they hand the tenant a defence or a counterclaim and they will be raised.
  2. Serve the notice. Form 3A, which is the version for private landlords (Form 3 is now for social housing), correctly completed, stating every ground relied on and the facts supporting each. The notice must be served in a way your tenancy agreement permits and you need proof of service.
  3. Wait the notice period. The clock runs from service, not from the date on the form. Getting this wrong by a day invalidates the notice.
  4. Issue the claim. If the tenant has not left, apply to the county court. The court fee for a possession claim is £415, on paper or online. Include the evidence for each ground in the particulars.
  5. The hearing. Typically six to twelve weeks after issue, depending on the court. The judge will consider the grounds, any defence and any counterclaim, and may adjourn if the paperwork is not in order.
  6. The order. Usually fourteen days to leave, extendable to six weeks in cases of exceptional hardship. On discretionary grounds the judge has wider power to postpone or suspend the order.
  7. Enforcement. If the tenant still does not leave, apply for a warrant, which costs £152, and a county court bailiff attends. Transferring to the High Court for a faster enforcement officer is possible but needs permission.

From notice to bailiffs, a straightforward arrears case takes five to seven months in most of England at the moment. Contested cases take longer.

Mistakes that lose cases

  • Using Form 3, the social housing form, instead of Form 3A, or an out-of-date version of it
  • Serving less notice than the ground requires, or miscounting the period
  • Relying on ground 8 alone when the arrears may fall below three months
  • No proof of service
  • A deposit that was never protected, or prescribed information never served, discovered at the hearing
  • Threatening the tenant, changing locks, cutting off services or turning up unannounced. Any of these is harassment under the Protection from Eviction Act 1977 and a criminal offence.

Key points

  • Every possession claim now needs a Section 8 ground, with evidence, on the prescribed notice
  • Selling or moving in needs four months' notice, and the notice cannot end inside the first twelve months
  • Mandatory arrears ground needs three months' arrears both when the notice is served and at the hearing
  • Misusing a ground, or re-letting too soon after using one, can mean a penalty of up to £40,000 and a rent repayment order
